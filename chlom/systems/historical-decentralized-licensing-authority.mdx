## Source basis

Early CHLOM whitepapers, Genesis/Technical Blueprint lineage and the recovered Help Center use **DLA** to describe a **Decentralized Licensing Authority**: a governance/issuer layer intended to verify rights, issue programmable licenses, enforce restrictions and coordinate license state.

Later CHLOM evolved `DLA` into **Dynamic Licensing Asset**. The historical authority function remains valuable IP, but the acronym is no longer used for authority in current CHLOM.

## Historical purpose

The historical architecture attempted to replace fragmented licensing gatekeepers with transparent, rules-driven licensing authority.

```text
RIGHTS HOLDER / SOURCE RIGHTS
          |
          v
DECENTRALIZED LICENSING AUTHORITY
   +------+-------+---------+
   |              |         |
verify rights   issue      govern
               license     state
   |              |         |
   +------+-------+---------+
          |
    DLA/LEX/TLaaS ecosystem
```

## Historical functions

Source-era DLA concepts include:

- verifying/recognizing licensor or issuer authority;
- registering licensable assets/rights;
- issuing digital/programmatic licenses;
- enforcing term, territory and transfer rules;
- validating participants/credentials;
- linking identity/Fingerprint records;
- interacting with compliance pre-checks;
- enabling LEX marketplace transactions;
- supporting revocation/renewal/transfer;
- linking royalty/distribution mechanics;
- preserving auditable license events.

## What this record does not claim

This article does **not** claim that:

- a decentralized autonomous legal authority currently exists;
- historical DLA has governmental/regulatory authority;
- public blockchain validators can create legal licensing authority;
- a token holder can issue rights they do not own/control;
- historical smart contracts are deployed today;
- current DLA means Decentralized Licensing Authority.

## Authority source

Even in a decentralized technical architecture, license authority must originate from valid rights or delegated authority.

```text
technical decentralization
   !=
source rights authority
```

A node, validator, DAO or smart contract cannot manufacture a copyright, trademark, contractual or other license right absent a valid source.

## Relationship to historical license tokens

Historical sources frequently represent licenses as NFTs/SBTs/tokens. The Decentralized Licensing Authority could authorize/issue those representations.

Current mapping separates:

```text
issuer authority
  -> Dynamic Licensing Asset semantic record
  -> optional token/provider representation
```

## Relationship to LEX

Historical LEX relied on DLA authority to ensure only eligible licenses entered the exchange.

```text
DLA authority verifies / issues
          |
          v
LEX lists / exchanges eligible licenses
```

Current CHLOM preserves this separation through rights validation, issuer stewardship and current DLA objects.

## Relationship to TLaaS

Historical Tokenized Licensing as a Service packages DLA/LEX/tokenized license capabilities for external platforms. The authority still depends on source rights.

## Relationship to identity

Historical DLA designs used DID/Fingerprint/credentials to verify issuers/licensees and support non-transferable or eligibility-restricted permissions.

```text
identity valid
   !=
rights authority
```

The issuer must satisfy both identity and rights authority.

## Governance/validator lineage

Source-era architecture contemplated validators, staking, DAO/council governance and adjudication mechanisms. Those mechanisms could confirm process/state under the protocol, but current interpretation preserves a key boundary:

```text
validator consensus != legal rights source
```

## Revocation/renewal lineage

The historical authority layer could coordinate license lifecycle actions. Modern CHLOM maps those actions to current Dynamic Licensing Asset lifecycle plus issuer/remedy authority.

## Failure modes in historical model

- issuer identity valid but rights invalid;
- validator consensus accepts fraudulent source rights;
- smart contract permits overly broad license;
- DAO votes to override rights it does not own;
- token transfer bypasses license restrictions;
- revocation fails to propagate;
- marketplace accepts unauthoritative license.

These failure modes directly motivate the current rights-before-license architecture.

## Threat model

- governance capture;
- validator collusion;
- fake chain-of-title;
- compromised issuer keys;
- unauthorized license minting;
- token/contract semantics diverging from legal agreement;
- smart-contract bugs;
- oracle/source manipulation;
- confusing decentralization with lawful authority.

## Test vectors for modernization

1. historical DLA issuer has no source rights -\> current mapping denies issuer authority;
2. historical token license exists but rights evidence absent -\> no current DLA activation;
3. validator quorum accepts state but agreement contradicts it -\> HOLD/source reconciliation;
4. issuer rights expire -\> modern issuer authority expires;
5. transfer is forbidden by source agreement -\> token/provider transfer cannot create current permission;
6. historical revocation record -\> preserve lineage, verify current state separately;
7. decentralized authority terminology appears in source -\> preserve as historical, do not rewrite to current DLA meaning;
8. current article references DLA -\> resolve Dynamic Licensing Asset unless explicitly historical.
